Module 1Basics

What is an identity?

Start with the real world. You are a person: an entity. You have things that point to you: your name, your employee ID, your passport number. Those are identifiers. You have facts about you: department, job title, date of birth, country. Those are attributes. And you have things that prove you're you: a passport photo, a signature, a door badge. Those are credentials.

A digital identity is the same four pieces, stored in a computer:

Entities come in two kinds

This is the distinction the whole modern field turns on:

Human identities

Employees, contractors, partners, customers. They log in interactively, can be asked for MFA, and leave the company one day.

Non-human identities (NHIs)

Service accounts, API keys, bots, CI/CD pipelines, cloud workloads, IoT devices, and now AI agents. They log in automatically, can't answer an MFA prompt, and often outlive the person who created them.

For decades, security teams focused on the first column. Today most organizations have far more machine identities than human ones (vendor surveys put the ratio anywhere from tens to over a hundred to one), and attackers have noticed. We'll spend all of Phase 4 on this.

Why identity became "the new perimeter"

Twenty years ago, companies protected a building-shaped network: a firewall at the edge, trusted users inside. Then came laptops that leave the office, SaaS apps that live on the internet, and cloud servers that aren't in any building. There's no longer a meaningful "inside". The only thing every access request still has in common is an identity asking for something. So identity became the control point. The flip side: stealing an identity is now the easiest way in. Attackers don't break in; they log in.

Analogy · the office building Think of your company as a building. The entity is you. Your identifier is the name printed on your badge. Your attributes are the floor and department encoded on it. Your credential is the badge's chip. Reception (the identity system) issued it. The turnstiles (applications) trust reception's badge instead of checking your passport themselves. We'll reuse this building all course long.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Attackers target whichever identity is weakest, and valid accounts (MITRE ATT&CK T1078) blend in with normal activity. The easiest target is often an unowned machine account nobody watches.

Detect: You can't detect misuse of an identity you don't know exists. An inventory with owners is the first detection control.

Defend: Keep a complete inventory of human and machine identities, each with an owner, and remove anything nobody claims.

  2. An API key is used by a program, not a person. There's nobody present to tap "approve" or insert a security key. Machine identities need different controls: short lifetimes, scoping, rotation, and binding to the workload.
  3. Users, apps and data are no longer inside one network, so the network edge can't be the gate. Every request still comes from an identity, so identity is where access is decided.

Module 2Basics

The four questions: identify, authenticate, authorize, audit

Every access decision, from a door turnstile to an AWS API call, runs through the same four steps. Security people often call them IAAA (or "AAA" when identification is folded into authentication).

StepQuestionIn the buildingIn software
IdentificationWho do you claim to be?"I'm Priya from Finance."Typing a username
Authentication (AuthN)Prove it.Tapping the badgePassword, passkey, certificate
Authorization (AuthZ)What may you do?Badge opens floor 3 onlyRoles, permissions, policies
Accounting / AuditWhat did you do?Turnstile logSign-in logs, audit trails
The mistake almost everyone makes early Authentication and authorization are different problems. AuthN answers "is this really Priya?" AuthZ answers "should Priya be able to approve this payment?" A system can get AuthN perfect (passkey, MFA) and still be breached because Priya had far more access than her job needed. Many real breaches are an authorization failure, not an authentication one.

Sessions and tokens: why you don't log in on every click

Authenticating is expensive (you'd hate typing a password every click), so after you prove yourself once, the system hands you a token or session cookie, a temporary pass that says "this person already authenticated". Each later request carries that token.

That's efficient, and it's also a huge security fact: whoever holds the token is treated as you. If malware steals your session cookie, the attacker skips the password and the MFA entirely. Remember this; it comes back in Module 16 (token theft) and Phase 4 (API keys are just very long-lived tokens).

Analogy · the wristband At a concert, security checks your ID once at the gate (authentication) and gives you a wristband (token). All night, staff only look at wristbands. Anyone who gets hold of your wristband walks in as you. A good wristband expires tonight; a bad one is valid for a year. API keys are often the year-long kind.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Every step is a target: usernames are enumerated (identification), credentials phished (authentication), roles abused (authorization), and logs cleared or avoided (audit).

Detect: Make sure IdP, directory, cloud and SaaS logs reach your SIEM and are kept long enough, ideally a year, because breaches are often found months later.

Defend: Treat logging as a security control: protect logs from tampering and alert when auditing is switched off.

Check yourself

  1. A user logs in with a passkey and then downloads the entire customer database, which they didn't need. Which of the four steps failed?
  2. Why is a stolen session cookie often worse than a stolen password?
  3. Which step lets you investigate an incident after the fact?
Answers
  1. Authorization. Authentication worked fine; the user simply had excessive access (a least-privilege failure).
  2. A cookie represents an already-completed authentication, including MFA. The attacker skips both.
  3. Accounting/audit. Without logs, you can't tell what an identity did.

User-to-user is really user → machine → user

Humans almost never authenticate each other directly; a system in the middle vouches. When an email arrives "from the CFO", the mail system checked that someone signed into that mailbox, and email standards (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) checked that it came from the company's domain. Nothing proved the human CFO wrote it. In early 2024, an employee of the engineering firm Arup in Hong Kong transferred about US$25 million after a video call in which the "CFO" and "colleagues" were deepfakes. The defense is old-fashioned: verify through a second, independent channel you already trust (call back a known number), and use digital signatures for documents that matter.

Delegation vs impersonation

When an assistant sends email for a boss, there are two ways to set it up. With delegation, the email says "Sam on behalf of Priya" and the logs show both people. With impersonation, Sam logs in as Priya (often with her password) and the logs show only Priya. Delegation keeps accountability; impersonation destroys it. The same choice appears for machines: a good design shows "expense app acting for Priya", not "Priya" or just "expense app".

Authentication should go both ways

We usually talk about the user proving themselves to the machine. But the machine should prove itself too: that's mutual authentication. Your browser checks a website's TLS certificate (a signed proof of who a server is — full mechanism in Module 4); Kerberos can do the same for a server proving itself back to a client (Module 8); in machine-to-machine traffic, mTLS ("mutual TLS": both sides show a certificate, not just one) does it for two services talking to each other. Phishing works precisely because humans can't reliably authenticate the website. Passkeys (Module 5) fix that by letting the device do the checking instead of the human.

The human-present test If a human is present, you can use MFA, passkeys and consent screens. If not, none of those work, and security depends on how the credential is stored, how short-lived it is, and whether the platform can vouch for the workload. This one fact is why machine identity (Phase 4) is a separate discipline.
Analogy · back to the building A visitor showing ID at reception is user→machine. Two colleagues on a speakerphone with someone claiming to be the CEO is user→user. The mail robot using the service lift with its own key card is machine→machine. A courier carrying your signed note authorizing them to collect one parcel is delegation. The note should name both of you and cover only that parcel.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Each pattern has a signature attack: phishing for user→machine, impersonation and deepfakes for user→user, secret theft for machine→machine, consent phishing for delegation.

Detect: Machine→machine traffic is predictable, so deviations are strong signals. User→user fraud usually shows up in process logs (a changed bank account) rather than identity logs.

Defend: Require independent verification for high-risk user→user requests (payments, bank-detail changes, MFA resets), and scope every delegation.

Module 4Basics

Credentials: what actually proves identity

Two questions classify every credential 1. Secret or key pair? A shared secret (password, API key) is known by both sides, so either side can leak it. With a key pair, only you hold the private key and others verify you with the public key; the server stores nothing secret.
2. Bearer or proof-of-possession? A bearer credential works for whoever presents it, like cash. A proof-of-possession credential signs a fresh challenge each time, so a copy of one login can't be replayed.

Shared secrets vs key pairs, from first principles

With a password, the server must keep something to compare against, so it keeps a hash. Steal the hash database and you can try to crack it. With public-key cryptography, the private key signs and the public key verifies. The server keeps only public keys, so stealing them gives an attacker nothing. That's why the whole industry is moving from secrets to key pairs: passkeys for humans, certificates and platform-issued keys for machines.

Primary vs derived credentials

You rarely present your primary credential (password, passkey — a device-held key pair, covered fully in Module 5) more than once. It gets exchanged for derived credentials: things the primary credential earns you, each one good enough to act as you without asking for the primary credential again. Three worked examples — don't worry about naming every link yet, each one gets its own full module later:

  • Web: passkey → a session with the service that checks logins for every app (an "identity provider," taught properly in Module 10) → a signed proof of that login handed to the app you're actually using (either a SAML assertion, Module 11, or an ID token, Module 12 — two different standards for the same idea) → the app's own session cookie.
  • Windows: password or Windows Hello → a Kerberos ticket-granting ticket (proof you logged into the network at all) → service tickets (a separate derived pass for each specific file share or app you touch) — both explained in Module 8.
  • APIs: client secret or certificate → an OAuth access token (minutes) and sometimes a refresh token (days) that can mint fresh access tokens without asking again — both explained in Module 12.

The pattern across all three: one primary credential, presented once, cashed in for a chain of derived passes. Stealing any link in that chain lets an attacker enter at that point, skipping every check that happened before it, including MFA — because a derived credential doesn't know or care how it was earned, only that it's valid. That's why token theft is such a big deal.

PKI in five sentences

A certificate is a signed statement from a Certificate Authority (CA): "this public key belongs to this name, valid until this date." Anyone who trusts the CA can check the statement without contacting the owner. That's how browsers trust websites, how smart cards log into Windows, and how services identify each other with mTLS. Certificates expire and can be revoked. Much of "machine identity management" is simply keeping thousands of certificates from expiring unnoticed or leaking.

Where credentials should live

  • On servers: slow, salted hashes of passwords, or only public keys.
  • On user devices: in hardware (TPM, Secure Enclave, smart card, YubiKey), where keys can't be copied out.
  • For machines: in a secrets manager, or better, no stored secret at all (workload identity, Module 24).
  • Never: in source code, chat messages, tickets, spreadsheets, or config files committed to git.
Analogy · the password vs the signet ring A shared secret is the password to a speakeasy: the doorman knows it too, so a crooked doorman can leak it. A key pair is a signet ring: anyone can check your wax seal against a public picture of it, but only you have the ring. A bearer token is a cloakroom ticket: whoever hands it over gets the coat.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Attackers harvest credentials where they rest (browser password stores via infostealers, LSASS memory, code repos, config files) and where they travel (phishing proxies).

Detect: Plant honeytokens: fake keys and accounts that nobody legitimately uses, so any use is a high-confidence alert.

Defend: Prefer credentials that are non-exportable (hardware-backed), short-lived and proof-of-possession over static bearer secrets.

Module 5Basics

Authentication and MFA in depth

The three factors

Something you know

Password, PIN, security question. Cheap, but can be guessed, reused, phished or leaked.

Something you have

Phone app, hardware key, smart card, a device's private key.

Something you are

Fingerprint, face. Usually unlocks a local key rather than being sent anywhere.

MFA (multi-factor authentication) means using two or more different factors. Two passwords is not MFA.

How passwords are stored (and why leaks hurt)

A well-built system never stores your password. It stores a hash: the output of a one-way function. At login it hashes what you typed and compares. Good systems use slow, salted hash functions (bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2) so a stolen database is expensive to crack. The real danger is reuse: when one site leaks, attackers try the same email/password pair everywhere. That's credential stuffing.

Why passkeys are phishing-resistant (first principles)

With a password or code, you decide whether a website is real, and humans are bad at that. With a passkey, your device holds a private key that was created for one specific website origin (say login.corp.com). When a site asks you to sign in, the browser tells the device which origin is asking. A fake site at login-corp.com gets nothing, because the device has no key for that origin. The human is taken out of the "is this site real?" decision. That's the entire trick, and it's why regulators and security teams push "phishing-resistant MFA".

Under the hood this is public-key cryptography: the website stores only your public key, so a server breach leaks nothing useful. FIDO2 is the standard; it's made of WebAuthn (the browser API) and CTAP (how the browser talks to a security key or phone). A "passkey" is the consumer-friendly name for a FIDO2 credential, often synced across your devices.

How an authenticator-app code is made

When you scan the QR code, your phone and the server both store the same random shared secret. From then on, each side independently computes:

code = last 6 digits of HMAC( shared_secret, current_time ÷ 30 seconds )

Same secret and same 30-second window give the same code, with nothing sent over the network. This is TOTP (time-based one-time password, RFC 6238). Two consequences: the code proves you have the secret right now, but it doesn't know which website you're typing it into, so a fake site can relay it. And if the QR code or secret leaks (a screenshot, a backup), the attacker can generate codes forever.

Push, number matching and MFA fatigue

Push MFA sends "Approve sign-in?" to your phone. Attackers who already have a password send dozens of prompts at night until a tired user taps Approve. Number matching shows a number on the login screen that you must type into the phone. You can't match a number you can't see, so blind approvals stop working. Microsoft and Okta now support or enforce it.

Where MFA can't reach

MFA is powerful, but it only protects the moment it's asked. Know the gaps:

  • Legacy protocols. Old email protocols (POP, IMAP, basic-auth SMTP), NTLM and some VPNs take just a username and password; there's no way to prompt for MFA. Attackers spray passwords at exactly these, which is why "block legacy authentication" is a standard control.
  • After login. The session cookie or token carries the result of MFA. Steal it and MFA never runs again.
  • Machines. Machine-to-machine calls have no human to prompt (Module 3).
  • Enrolment and recovery. Whoever can register a new MFA method, or get one reset, owns the account. Attackers who phish a password often immediately register their own authenticator. Protect MFA registration and help-desk resets as tightly as login itself.
  • Inside the Windows network. Once you're logged into a Windows PC, access to file shares and intranet sites uses Kerberos tickets without asking again (Module 8). MFA usually happens only at the first sign-in, through Windows Hello or a smart card, unless extra products are added.

Step-up authentication and MFA signals

Step-up means asking for stronger proof at a sensitive moment, such as approving a payment or opening an admin console, even mid-session. With single sign-on (one login covering many apps, the full mechanism is Module 10), MFA happens once at the identity provider — the one service that checks logins on everyone else's behalf, taught properly in Module 10 — which then tells the app how you authenticated: SAML carries it in the AuthnContext (Module 11) and OpenID Connect in the amr and acr claims (Module 12). An app can refuse a session that wasn't created with MFA.

Adaptive (risk-based) authentication

Modern identity providers don't treat every login the same. They look at signals (new device? impossible travel? known-bad IP? managed laptop?) and decide whether to allow, step up to stronger MFA, or block. Microsoft calls this Conditional Access; Okta calls them authentication policies. Same idea.

Analogy · the bouncer and the seal A password is telling the bouncer a secret word; anyone who overheard it can repeat it. A passkey is like a wax seal that only fits one particular door. Even if a crook builds a fake door that looks identical, your seal simply doesn't fit, so there's nothing for them to steal.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Phishing-as-a-service kits automate adversary-in-the-middle attacks against password + OTP. Also common: MFA fatigue, SIM swaps, and registering the attacker's own MFA method right after a phish.

Detect: Alert on a new MFA method registered soon after a risky sign-in, bursts of denied push prompts, and legacy-protocol sign-ins.

Defend: Phishing-resistant MFA for admins first, then everyone; block legacy authentication; require a managed device for sensitive apps; protect MFA registration and recovery.

Authorization in depth

Authorization is deciding whether subject may perform action on resource, perhaps under some condition. "Can Priya (subject) approve (action) invoice #88 (resource) from the office network (condition)?" Every authorization model is a different way of storing and evaluating that sentence.

The main models

Four principles you'll hear constantly

  • Least privilege: give only the access needed for the task, nothing more.
  • Need to know: least privilege applied to information.
  • Separation of duties (SoD): no one person should be able to both create and approve a payment. This is a big deal for SOX audits.
  • Just-in-time (JIT) access: grant access when needed, for a limited time, then remove it automatically. The opposite is standing access, which is always on and always stealable.

Coarse vs fine-grained

Coarse-grained authorization answers "can Priya use the finance app at all?", usually decided at login by the identity provider (the login-checking service from Module 5, covered fully in Module 10). Fine-grained authorization answers "can Priya approve this invoice over $10k?", usually decided inside the application. Identity teams typically own the first; developers own the second. Many gaps live in between.

Privilege creep People change jobs internally and keep their old access, so over years they accumulate far more than they need. This silent accumulation is one of the most common findings in any identity audit, and it's the reason access reviews exist (Module 13).
Security lens

Attacker's view: Privilege escalation through misconfiguration: nested groups, over-broad roles, identities that can edit their own permissions. In applications, broken object-level authorization (changing an ID in a URL to see someone else's data) tops the OWASP API Security Top 10.

Detect: Alert on assignments to privileged roles and changes to admin groups; review who can grant access, not just who has it.

Defend: Least privilege, separation of duties, just-in-time elevation, and authorization tests in every application.

Directories and the source of truth

A directory is a database of identities and their attributes, optimized for lookups: "who is priya@corp.com, what groups is she in?" It's the phone book the whole company trusts.

The key players

  • LDAP is a protocol (and data model) for querying directories. Old, still everywhere.
  • Active Directory (AD) is Microsoft's on-premises directory. It runs most corporate Windows networks: user accounts, computers, groups, and Group Policy. It authenticates with Kerberos (and the older NTLM).
  • Microsoft Entra ID (formerly Azure AD) is Microsoft's cloud identity service. Despite the old name, it is not AD in the cloud: it speaks modern web protocols (SAML, OAuth, OIDC) rather than Kerberos and LDAP. Most enterprises run a hybrid: AD on-prem, synced to Entra ID.
  • Cloud IdPs such as Okta, Ping Identity, Google Workspace and JumpCloud play a similar role, sometimes sitting in front of AD.

How Active Directory actually checks who you are, through Kerberos and NTLM, gets its own module next.

Authoritative sources

Who decides that Priya exists, is in Finance and starts Monday? Not IT. The HR system (Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, etc.) is usually the authoritative source for employees. Contractors often come from a vendor-management system. A mature identity program flows attributes from these sources into the directory automatically, so the directory reflects reality without anyone typing.

Windows authentication: NTLM and Kerberos

When you log into a corporate Windows laptop and then open a file share or intranet site, no password prompt appears. Two protocols make that happen: Kerberos (the default) and NTLM (the legacy fallback). Most ransomware gangs move through a network by abusing these two, so they're worth learning step by step.

NTLM: challenge and response

Your password never crosses the network. What's sent is a response computed from the server's random challenge and your NT hash (a hash of your password). The design has deep flaws:

  • The hash is as good as the password. The NT hash is unsalted, so anyone holding it can compute valid responses without ever knowing the password. This is pass-the-hash. Attackers dump hashes from the memory of machines where admins have logged in, using tools like Mimikatz.
  • No server authentication, so relay attacks. The client never checks who it's talking to. An attacker in the middle forwards your challenge and response to another server and logs in there as you (NTLM relay). Tricks such as PetitPotam can even force a server to authenticate to the attacker.
  • Crackable. Captured NTLMv1 responses fall quickly; NTLMv2 is better but weak passwords still get cracked offline.
  • No MFA and no way to limit delegation.

Status: Microsoft has deprecated NTLM, and the newest Windows versions have removed NTLMv1. In practice NTLM lingers wherever Kerberos can't work: connections by IP address, old applications, devices outside the domain. The defensive plan is to audit NTLM usage, turn on SMB signing and LDAP signing with channel binding (which break relay), add high-value users to the Protected Users group, and switch NTLM off system by system.

Kerberos: tickets from a trusted third party

Kerberos is named after the three-headed dog of Greek myth, because it has three parties: the client, the Key Distribution Center (KDC) that runs on every domain controller, and the service you want. The founding idea: everyone shares a secret with the KDC and with no one else. The KDC holds a key for every user and service account (derived from their passwords) plus the key of a special account called krbtgt, which it uses to seal its own tickets.

  1. AS-REQ. Your PC sends your username and a timestamp encrypted with a key derived from your password (called pre-authentication).
  2. AS-REP. The KDC checks it and returns a Ticket-Granting Ticket (TGT) sealed with the krbtgt key, which your PC can't read, plus a session key.
  3. TGS-REQ. When you open \\fs01\finance, your PC shows the TGT and asks for a ticket to the service named cifs/fs01.corp.com. That name is a Service Principal Name (SPN), which the directory maps to an account.
  4. TGS-REP. The KDC returns a service ticket sealed with the file server's own key.
  5. AP-REQ. Your PC hands the ticket to the file server, which opens it with its own key. Inside is a PAC (Privilege Attribute Certificate) listing your group memberships, which drives authorization.
  6. AP-REP. Optionally the server proves its identity back: mutual authentication, which NTLM lacks.

Other facts worth knowing: tickets last about 10 hours by default; clocks must agree within 5 minutes; smart cards and Windows Hello for Business replace the password in step 1 (PKINIT), which is where strong MFA enters. Delegation lets a web server fetch tickets on your behalf to reach a database. That's Kerberos's version of "machine on behalf of a user" (Module 3). Constrained delegation limits where; unconstrained delegation is dangerous because the server keeps copies of users' TGTs.

The identity lifecycle

Identities aren't static. The core lifecycle is Joiner → Mover → Leaver (JML):

  • Joiner: a new person needs an account and "birthright" access (email, chat, HR portal) on day one.
  • Mover: someone changes teams. They need new access and must lose the old access. This step is where privilege creep is born.
  • Leaver: someone leaves. All access must be removed promptly, everywhere, including SaaS apps nobody told IT about.

Provisioning and SCIM

Provisioning is creating and updating accounts in target systems; deprovisioning is removing them. Doing it by hand, via tickets, doesn't scale and gets forgotten. SCIM (System for Cross-domain Identity Management) is the standard API that lets your IdP automatically create, update and disable accounts in apps like Slack, Salesforce or GitHub.

Orphaned accounts When a leaver's account survives in some app, it becomes an orphan: active, unmonitored, and a gift to attackers. Colonial Pipeline (2021) was breached through a VPN account that was no longer in use and had no MFA. The lifecycle for machines is even weaker: service accounts rarely have a "leaver" event at all (Phase 4).
Security lens

Attacker's view: Orphaned and dormant accounts, leavers with lingering SaaS access, contractors with no end date, and rehires who get their old access back.

Detect: Reconcile HR against the directory every day; alert on any sign-in by a terminated user and on reactivation of dormant accounts.

Defend: Automated, HR-driven deprovisioning with a same-day target; mandatory end dates for contractors; disable accounts after a period of inactivity.

  2. What problem does SCIM solve?
Answers
  1. Mover. Granting new access is visible (the person asks for it); removing old access is invisible, so nobody asks.
  2. Automated, standard provisioning and deprovisioning between an IdP and apps, instead of manual tickets or custom scripts per app.

Single sign-on, from first principles

Without SSO, every app keeps its own passwords: 100 apps means 100 passwords, 100 login pages to phish, and 100 places to remember when someone leaves. The first-principles fix is to separate proving who you are from using the app. Apps outsource authentication to one trusted Identity Provider (IdP). Each app becomes a Service Provider (SP), or Relying Party (RP), that accepts the IdP's signed statement: "I verified this is Priya, at 9:02, with a passkey."

Federation is that trust across organizational boundaries: your company's IdP vouches for you to Salesforce, a different company. The trust is set up in advance by exchanging metadata and signing keys, so at login time the app only has to check a signature.

What SSO is not

  • Same sign-on (password sync): the same password works in many apps, but you still type it into each one, and each one sees it. Convenient, not SSO.
  • Password vaulting: a browser extension fills in stored passwords for apps that can't do real SSO. Useful for legacy apps, but the passwords still exist and can still leak.
  • LDAP login: an app asks for your directory password and checks it against LDAP itself. The app sees your password and usually can't do MFA. Treat it as a warning sign.

Sessions come in layers

After SSO there are at least three sessions: the device session (your Windows logon), the IdP session (your Okta or Entra cookie), and one app session per app. Logging out of one app doesn't end the others, and "single logout" across all of them is unreliable in practice. That matters for offboarding: disabling someone in the IdP stops new logins, but existing app sessions and refresh tokens can live on for hours or days. You also need session revocation, SCIM deprovisioning (Module 9) and, ideally, real-time signals (Module 18).

SP-initiated vs IdP-initiated

SP-initiated: you open the app, it sends you to the IdP with a request, and it later checks the response matches that request. IdP-initiated: you click a tile on the IdP dashboard and the IdP sends an unsolicited response to the app. IdP-initiated is convenient but weaker, because the app has no request to match the response against, which makes some injection and replay attacks easier. Prefer SP-initiated where apps support it.

Provisioning: JIT vs SCIM

Many apps can create an account the first time someone arrives with a valid SSO response (just-in-time provisioning). But SSO can't remove accounts, because a leaver simply never shows up again. That's why SSO should be paired with SCIM.

The trade-off SSO gives you fewer passwords, MFA in one place, one audit trail and a single off-switch. It also makes the IdP the master key: compromise the IdP, its admins or its signing keys, and every connected app opens. Protect IdP administrators with phishing-resistant MFA and privileged-access controls.
Analogy · reception's stamp SSO is the badge from reception. Every turnstile trusts reception's stamp instead of checking your passport. That's efficient and much safer than every door keeping its own guest list, until someone steals reception's stamp. Then every door opens.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Attack the IdP itself: take over an admin account, add a malicious identity provider or federated domain for persistence, steal IdP session cookies, or use apps that still allow local (non-SSO) logins.

Detect: Alert on federation and routing-rule changes, new admin role grants, new app credentials, and app sign-ins with no matching IdP sign-in.

Defend: Phishing-resistant MFA and PAWs for IdP admins, very few super-admins, configuration-change monitoring, and local logins disabled wherever SSO exists.

SAML in depth

SAML 2.0 (Security Assertion Markup Language, an OASIS standard from 2005) still runs most enterprise SaaS single sign-on. It's XML-based and designed for web browsers.

Step 0: trust is set up before anyone logs in

The IdP and the app exchange metadata, usually an XML file or a copy-paste in the admin console:

  • IdP → app: the IdP's entity ID (its unique name), its sign-in URL, and its signing certificate (public key).
  • App → IdP: the app's entity ID and its ACS URL (Assertion Consumer Service: where to send the response), plus which identifier and attributes it needs.

The login flow (SP-initiated)

Anatomy of an assertion (simplified)

<saml:Assertion ID="_a1b2c3" IssueInstant="2026-09-28T09:00:00Z">
  <saml:Issuer>https://idp.corp.com</saml:Issuer>
  <ds:Signature>… signed with the IdP's private key …</ds:Signature>
  <saml:Subject>
    <saml:NameID>priya@corp.com</saml:NameID>

  </saml:Conditions>

What the app must check

  1. The signature is valid under the IdP certificate it already trusts, and the signed element is the one it actually reads.
  2. The Issuer is the expected IdP.
  3. The Audience is this app.
  4. The current time is within NotBefore and NotOnOrAfter.
  5. The response answers a request it really sent (InResponseTo) and went to its own ACS URL.
  6. The assertion ID hasn't been seen before (replay protection).

How SAML gets attacked

  • Weak signature checking: the app accepts unsigned assertions, or trusts whatever certificate is embedded in the message. Then anyone can write their own assertion.
  • XML Signature Wrapping: the attacker keeps the genuine signed element but adds a forged one; a sloppy parser verifies the first and reads the second.
  • Comment injection (2018): researchers found several SAML libraries read priya@corp.com<!---->.evil.com one way when checking the signature and another way when extracting the username.
  • Golden SAML: steal the IdP's token-signing private key (historically from on-prem AD FS servers) and mint valid assertions for anyone, skipping passwords and MFA entirely. It was used in the SolarWinds campaign (2020). Defenses: keep signing keys in an HSM or a cloud IdP, restrict IdP admin access, and alert on app logins with no matching IdP sign-in.
  • Certificate expiry: signing certificates expire. If nobody tracks them, SSO for every app breaks on the same morning.

SAML or OpenID Connect?

SAML is mature and universal in enterprise SaaS, but it's XML-heavy, browser-only and has no API tokens. OIDC uses JSON and JWTs, works well for mobile apps and single-page apps, and pairs naturally with OAuth for API access. New builds usually choose OIDC; you'll still be supporting SAML for years.

Security lens

Attacker's view: Golden SAML (a stolen signing key), signature-validation bugs, and XML signature wrapping.

Detect: App sign-ins without a matching IdP authentication event; changes to token-signing certificates; exports of AD FS configuration.

Defend: Keep signing keys in an HSM or with a cloud IdP, use well-maintained SAML libraries, and prefer SP-initiated flows (Module 19).

OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect

SAML handles browser login for enterprise web apps. The modern web also needs apps to call APIs, mobile apps to sign users in, and machines to talk to machines. That's the job of OAuth 2.0, and of OpenID Connect (OIDC), which is built on top of it.

The most important sentence in this module OAuth is not an authentication protocol. An OAuth access token says "the bearer may call this API with these scopes"; it does not reliably say who the user is. OpenID Connect adds an ID token for that. Mixing them up has caused real vulnerabilities.

The OIDC authorization code flow

JWTs: the tokens you'll see everywhere

A JSON Web Token is three base64url chunks joined by dots: header.payload.signature. The payload holds claims: iss (issuer), sub (subject), aud (audience), exp (expiry), plus things like email or groups. The signature lets anyone with the IdP's public key verify it wasn't tampered with. It's signed, not encrypted: anyone can read it, so never put secrets in a JWT.

{ "iss": "https://login.corp.com",
  "sub": "00u1a2b3c",
  "aud": "finance-app",
  "email": "priya@corp.com",
  "exp": 1790000000 }

OAuth grant types to know

  • Authorization code + PKCE: a user is present (the flow above). The default for web, mobile and single-page apps.
  • Client credentials: no user; a machine authenticates as itself with a client ID and a secret or certificate. This is the standard machine-to-machine pattern (Module 3) and a non-human identity, which will matter a lot in Phase 4.
  • Token exchange / on-behalf-of: a backend swaps the user's token for a new one to call a downstream API, so the user's identity travels down the chain instead of being lost.
  • Refresh tokens: long-lived tokens that get new access tokens without re-login. Very valuable to attackers.
  • Device code: for TVs and CLIs ("go to this URL and enter code ABCD"). Also abused in phishing.
Analogy · the valet key OAuth is a valet key: it lets the valet drive your car but not open the trunk, and you never hand over your house keys. OpenID Connect adds a signed note from the valet company saying "this valet is Sam". SAML is an older, more formal letter of introduction written in XML.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Consent phishing, device-code phishing (used heavily by state actors in 2024–25), refresh-token theft, redirect-URI manipulation, tokens accepted for the wrong audience, and leaked client secrets.

Detect: New consents with high-risk scopes such as Mail.Read and offline_access; device-code sign-ins by users who never use them; new secrets added to app registrations.

Defend: Admin-consent workflow, block device-code flow where it isn't needed, PKCE everywhere, exact redirect-URI matching, sender-constrained tokens, and certificates or federation instead of client secrets.

Identity governance and administration (IGA)

IAM grants access. Governance asks whether that access is still right, and proves it to auditors. IGA platforms (SailPoint, Saviynt, Omada, Microsoft Entra ID Governance, and others) typically do:

  • Access requests with approval workflows: "Priya requests SAP-AP-Approver; manager and app owner approve."
  • Access reviews / certifications: periodically, managers confirm each person's access is still needed. Required by SOX, ISO 27001, PCI DSS and others.
  • Separation-of-duties policies: flag or block toxic combinations (create vendor + pay vendor).
  • Role management and role mining: discover which permissions naturally cluster together.
  • Automated JML provisioning across hundreds of apps, driven by HR.
Rubber-stamping A manager with 40 reports and 30 apps each sees 1,200 lines to review and clicks "approve all". The certification happened on paper and achieved nothing. Modern IGA fights this with risk-based reviews (only show unusual or unused access), usage data ("this person hasn't used this in 180 days"), and AI-assisted recommendations.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Attackers love dormant privileged access; insiders exploit separation-of-duties gaps for fraud.

Detect: Access reviews are a detective control. Feed their findings (unused admin rights, toxic combinations) to the security team, not just to auditors.

Defend: Risk-based reviews focused on privileged and unused access, enforced SoD rules, and identity risk scores shared with the SOC.

Privileged access management (PAM) and privileged identity management (PIM)

Some identities can do catastrophic things: domain admins, root on servers, cloud administrators, database owners, and the service accounts behind them. These are privileged accounts. Attackers who get in with a normal account spend their time hunting for one.

PAM and PIM: two questions about the same problem

Both terms sit in the same space, and vendors don't always use them consistently -- that's worth knowing going in, not glossing over. But there's a real, useful distinction underneath the overlap:

  • PAM asks: "how is this privileged account's credential protected, every time it's used?" Vaulting the password, rotating it, recording the session, injecting the credential so a human never sees it. PAM is built around the account and the session.
  • PIM asks: "who currently holds this privileged role, and for how long?" Instead of a person being a permanent admin, they're eligible for the role and activate it for a time-boxed window, with a justification and often an approval. PIM is built around the identity's standing state -- are they privileged right now, or just eligible to become privileged.
Analogy · the spare key vs. the guest list PAM is the locked box the spare key lives in: it controls who can take the key out, logs when they did, and changes the lock after. PIM is the guest list for a private room: most people aren't on it at all; being "eligible" means you can ask to be let in for the next hour, with a reason, and you're off the list again once the hour's up.

The clearest real implementation of PIM specifically is Microsoft Entra ID's own Privileged Identity Management feature: a user can be made eligible for Global Administrator without being an active admin day to day; when they need it, they activate the role for a set duration, sometimes with an approver and MFA required at activation. That's PIM's core mechanism: eligible vs. active, and time-bound activation, applied to roles rather than to a shared credential.

In practice the two work together: PIM decides who is currently allowed to act as an admin at all; PAM protects the credential and session once they do. A mature program has both, and the goal common to each is the same one named below: zero standing privilege.

What PAM tools do

  • Vaulting: privileged passwords and keys live in a vault, not in spreadsheets or people's heads.
  • Rotation: the vault changes those passwords automatically, often after every use.
  • Session brokering and recording: admins connect through the PAM tool, which injects the credential (the admin never sees it) and records the session.
  • Just-in-time elevation: admin rights are granted for an approved window, then removed. The goal is zero standing privilege.
  • Endpoint privilege management: removing local admin rights from laptops and elevating only specific tasks.

Traditional vendors: CyberArk, BeyondTrust, Delinea. Cloud platforms have their own JIT tools (for example, Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management). Many newer tools focus on JIT cloud access.

Real-world lesson · Uber, 2022 An attacker got a contractor's password, then spammed MFA push requests until the contractor accepted. Inside the network they found a script on a file share with hardcoded admin credentials for the PAM system itself. One human weakness plus one embedded machine credential gave them broad access. Both themes, human MFA and machine secrets, recur throughout this course.
Security lens

Attacker's view: The vault itself is a prime target (the Uber case). Other routes: hijacking admin sessions, and "shadow admins" (accounts with admin-equivalent rights outside the admin groups).

Detect: Privileged access that bypasses PAM (direct RDP with an admin account), vault configuration changes, and use of break-glass accounts.

Defend: Put the PAM platform in Tier 0 (the walled-off control-plane tier covered in Module 17), require phishing-resistant MFA on the vault, hunt for shadow admins with attack-path tools, and aim for zero standing privilege.

Check yourself

  1. What does "zero standing privilege" mean?

  3. A user is "eligible" for an admin role in Entra PIM but hasn't activated it. Are they currently privileged?
Answers
  1. No identity holds privileged access permanently; it's granted just-in-time and expires.
  2. So the credential can't be copied, reused later, or phished from the admin, and so it can be rotated freely.
  3. No -- eligibility is not activation. They hold no standing privilege until they activate the role for a time-boxed window; eligibility just means they're allowed to ask.

Attackers think in graphs

A well-known line from Microsoft's John Lambert: "Defenders think in lists. Attackers think in graphs." A defender sees a list of 5,000 accounts. An attacker sees a graph: this user is logged into that server, where an admin also has a session, whose group can reset passwords for the Domain Admins group. Tools like BloodHound (open source, from SpecterOps) collect those relationships from Active Directory and Entra ID and compute the shortest path to Domain Admin or Global Admin. Defenders now use the same tools to find and cut those paths before attackers do.

Tier 0: the crown jewels

Tier 0 is anything that controls the identity of everything else. If an attacker controls any Tier 0 asset, they control the whole company. The list is longer than most people expect:

  • Domain controllers, and anything that can back them up or restore them
  • AD FS or any other server holding token-signing keys
  • Microsoft Entra Connect (the sync server), which can write passwords both ways
  • AD Certificate Services (the company's internal CA)
  • The PAM vault and its admins
  • IdP super-admins and Global Administrators
  • Device-management admins who can push code to every machine, including admins' machines

Microsoft's enterprise access model formalizes this: Tier 0 accounts only ever log into Tier 0 systems, from dedicated admin workstations, so their credentials are never left behind on an ordinary laptop.

Persistence is identity Look at stage 3 again: almost every persistence technique is an identity artifact. That's why "we reset the user's password" rarely ends an intrusion. The attacker's MFA method, OAuth app, refresh tokens and forwarding rules all survive a password reset (Module 21).

Module 18Advanced

Defending identity: Zero Trust, ITDR, ISPM

Zero Trust

Zero Trust (defined in NIST SP 800-207) replaces "trust anything inside the network" with never trust, always verify. Every request is evaluated on identity, device health and context, gets the least privilege necessary, and is re-evaluated continuously. Identity is the main input to every Zero Trust decision.

The defensive stack, as layers

  1. Strong authentication: phishing-resistant MFA for everyone, starting with admins; passwordless where possible; hardened recovery.
  2. Context-aware access: conditional access policies on device, location and risk.
  3. Least privilege: JIT for admins, access reviews, remove dormant accounts.
  4. Posture management (ISPM): Identity Security Posture Management continuously scans your IdPs, directories and cloud for misconfigurations: accounts without MFA, stale admins, risky OAuth grants, over-permissioned service accounts. Think "vulnerability scanning, but for identity".
  5. Detection and response (ITDR): Identity Threat Detection and Response watches identity activity for attacks in progress (impossible travel, Kerberoasting, token replay, suspicious consent grants) and responds by revoking sessions, forcing re-auth, or disabling accounts. Gartner coined the term in 2022.
  6. Continuous signals: the OpenID Shared Signals Framework (with CAEP and RISC) lets systems tell each other in real time "this session is now risky, revoke it", instead of waiting for a token to expire.
Analogy · the building again Zero Trust means every door checks your badge, not just the front entrance. ISPM is the facilities audit that finds doors propped open and badges that never expire. ITDR is the guard watching cameras who notices someone badging into five floors in two minutes and deactivates the badge.

Check yourself

Answers
  1. ISPM: it's a standing weakness (posture), not an active attack.
  2. ITDR: it's an active threat to detect and respond to.

Protecting the identity control plane

The systems that issue identity (IdPs, directories, signing keys, sync servers, vaults) are the highest-value targets in any company, because they can mint access to everything else. First principle: protect the thing that makes keys more than any single door.

Case studies

  • Storm-0558 (2023): a China-based actor obtained a Microsoft consumer-account signing key and, because of a token-validation flaw, forged tokens that enterprise Exchange Online accepted. Identity incident response

Evict, don't just reset An attacker's access is the sum of every credential, token and persistence mechanism they've gathered. If you remove only some of them, they come back through the rest. Containment has to take everything away at once.

Playbook: compromised user account

  1. Contain: block sign-in; revoke all sessions and refresh tokens (a password reset alone doesn't kill existing tokens); reset the password.
  2. Remove persistence: MFA methods the attacker registered, OAuth consents and app passwords, mailbox rules and forwarding, newly registered devices, personal access tokens, SSH keys, any accounts or apps the attacker created.
  3. Scope: use sign-in and audit logs to find everything the account touched, what data was accessed, and whether the attacker moved to other identities.
  4. Recover: verify the real user out-of-band, re-enrol them with phishing-resistant MFA, and clean or reimage their device if an infostealer is suspected.
  5. Learn: find and fix the root cause (a missing policy, a legacy protocol, the help-desk process).

Other playbooks

Compromised service account or NHIRotate its credentials; review everything it can reach; check for new credentials added to it; decide whether it should exist at all.

In a big AD compromise, tip off the attacker by cleaning up piecemeal and they may launch ransomware early. Scope quietly, then evict in one coordinated move.

How NHIs break

OWASP published a Non-Human Identities Top 10 (2025). It's the best one-page map of the risks:

  1. Improper offboarding: NHIs left active after the app or project is gone.
  2. Secret leakage: keys in source code, logs, tickets, chat messages, container images.
  3. Vulnerable third-party NHIs: an integration vendor gets breached and their tokens into your systems go with it.
  4. Insecure authentication: deprecated or weak mechanisms.
  5. Overprivileged NHIs.
  6. Insecure cloud deployment configurations (e.g., CI/CD using static secrets instead of federation).
  7. Long-lived secrets.
  8. Environment isolation failures: the same credential in test and production.
  9. NHI reuse: one identity shared by many apps, so you can't scope or attribute it.
  10. Human use of NHIs: people logging in as service accounts, bypassing MFA and audit.

Incidents that shaped the field

  • Cloudflare (late 2023): after the Okta support breach, Cloudflare rotated credentials, but missed one access token and a few service account credentials. An attacker used them weeks later. Lesson: rotation must be complete and verifiable.
  • Internet Archive (2024): reported access traced to an exposed, unrotated GitLab token.
  • tj-actions/changed-files (2025): a widely used GitHub Action was compromised to dump CI/CD secrets into build logs, exposing secrets for many repositories at once. Lesson: your pipeline's dependencies can read your pipeline's secrets.
  • Salesloft Drift (2025): attackers stole OAuth tokens belonging to the Drift chat integration and used them to pull data from many companies' Salesforce instances. Lesson: a third-party integration is an identity with standing access into your data (OWASP NHI3).
Pattern Secrets are copyable, bearer-style credentials. If a secret exists, assume it can leak. The durable fix is not "hide secrets better" but "have fewer, shorter-lived secrets, each with a known owner and narrow scope". That's Module 24.

  1. Improper offboarding (the token outlives its owner), human use of NHIs (a human token used as a service identity), and likely long-lived secrets.
  2. They had granted a third party's integration standing OAuth access to their Salesforce data. When the third party's tokens were stolen, that access went with them.
Module 24Advanced

Securing workloads and secrets

Think of NHI security as a maturity ladder. Each rung reduces how many long-lived secrets exist.

RungWhat it looks like
0 · ChaosKeys hardcoded in code and config; nobody knows how many exist.
1 · DiscoverSecret scanning in repos and CI (GitHub secret scanning, GitGuardian, TruffleHog); inventory of service accounts, OAuth apps and keys, each with an owner.
2 · VaultSecrets move into a secrets manager (HashiCorp Vault, AWS Secrets Manager, Azure Key Vault, GCP Secret Manager, Akeyless, CyberArk); apps fetch at runtime; automated rotation.
3 · Dynamic secretsThe vault issues a fresh database credential per job that expires in minutes.
4 · Secretless (workload identity)The workload proves what it is using its platform, and receives a short-lived token. No stored secret at all.

Workload identity federation, from first principles

Here's the key insight. A human proves who they are with a passkey. How can a program prove who it is without a stored secret? Answer: the platform it runs on vouches for it. GitHub Actions can issue a signed OIDC token saying "this is repo corp/payments, branch main, workflow deploy". AWS, Azure or GCP is configured to trust GitHub's signature and exchange that token for short-lived cloud credentials scoped to exactly that repo and branch. Nothing long-lived to steal.

The same pattern appears everywhere: AWS IAM roles for EC2 and Lambda, Azure managed identities, GKE and EKS workload identity, and SPIFFE/SPIRE, an open standard (a CNCF project) that gives every workload a cryptographic identity (an "SVID", as an X.509 certificate or JWT) based on attested properties of where it's running.

Other controls

  • Ownership: every NHI must have a human owner and a purpose. No owner means candidate for removal.
  • Least privilege, measured: compare granted permissions against permissions actually used; trim the difference.
  • Behavioural baselines: NHIs are predictable, so a service account calling a new API from a new IP stands out. This is ITDR for machines.
  • Third-party app governance: review OAuth apps connected to Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Salesforce, GitHub and Slack; revoke unused grants.
  • Certificate lifecycle management: public TLS certificate maximum lifetimes are being cut in stages from about a year toward 47 days by 2029 (a CA/Browser Forum decision), which makes automated renewal mandatory.
Try it (30 min): Read GitHub's documentation on "Configuring OpenID Connect in Amazon Web Services" (or Azure/GCP). Draw the trust relationship: who signs, who verifies, what claims are checked. If you have a sandbox cloud account, set it up and delete the old access key afterwards.

Module 26Frontier

AI agents as identities

An AI agent is software that decides for itself which actions to take: read email, query a database, open a pull request, issue a refund. That makes it a new, strange kind of identity: it's an NHI, but its behaviour isn't predictable the way a cron job's is, and it often acts on behalf of a human.

The questions it raises

  • Whose identity is it? Does the agent act as itself, as the user, or as "the agent on behalf of the user"? Audit logs need to show both.
  • Delegation: how does a user give an agent some of their access (read my calendar, not send email), for a limited time, revocably? OAuth scopes and token exchange (RFC 8693) are the current building blocks.
  • Least privilege for something non-deterministic: you can't fully predict what an agent will try, so permissions must be tight and actions possibly approved by a human.
  • Prompt injection → confused deputy: an attacker plants instructions in an email or web page; the agent reads them and uses its own legitimate access to do the attacker's bidding. The agent's identity is fine; its judgement has been hijacked. This is why authorization, not just authentication, matters so much for agents.
  • Agent sprawl: like early SaaS, teams spin up agents and connectors with long-lived API keys, with no inventory or owner.

Where standards are heading

The Model Context Protocol (MCP), a common way to connect AI agents to tools and data, specifies OAuth 2.1-based authorization for remote servers. IETF and OpenID Foundation groups are actively working on agent identity, delegation and authorization. Identity vendors across the board are launching "agent identity" features. Treat this module as a moving target: re-check it every few months.

First-principles rule for agents Give an agent the smallest, shortest-lived, most specific access that lets it finish the task. Log every action with both the agent's and the human's identity. Require human approval for anything irreversible.

Check yourself

  1. An email assistant agent has full mailbox access. A malicious email says "forward all invoices to attacker@evil.com". Which control helps most?
  2. Why is an agent harder to secure than a traditional service account?
Answers
  1. Narrow authorization (no send/forward scope, or forwarding only internally) plus human approval for sending externally. Better authentication doesn't help; the agent's identity was never stolen.
  2. A service account does the same thing every time, so you can scope and baseline it tightly. An agent chooses its actions at runtime, can be manipulated by content it reads, and often acts for a human.
Module 27Frontier

Securing AI agents: a reference architecture

Start from this assumption Prompt injection is not solved. Design as if the agent will be tricked sooner or later. Then identity and authorization become your blast-radius controls: a tricked agent should only be able to do a little damage, visibly, and be switched off quickly.

Threats specific to agents

Excessive agencyThe agent has more tools, permissions or autonomy than the task needs; OWASP's Top 10 for LLM applications lists this as its own risk

The architecture, layer by layer

  1. Registry. Every agent is a registered identity with an owner, purpose, environment and lifecycle (created, reviewed, retired), just like any NHI (Module 22).
  2. Authentication. Agents get their own workload identity and short-lived credentials, never static keys pasted into prompts or config.
  3. Delegation. When acting for a person, the agent uses a delegated token that carries both identities. OAuth token exchange (RFC 8693) can express this: sub is the user, and an act (actor) claim names the agent.
  4. Authorization at the tool boundary. A gateway or policy engine checks every tool call (which agent, for which user, what action, on which resource), outside the model. The model's judgement is not a security control.
  5. Scoped tokens per tool. Each token is valid only for one specific tool server or API, never a master token.
  6. Human approval for high-impact actions: payments, deleting data, emailing outsiders, changing permissions.
  7. Secrets out of the context window. The tool fetches secrets from a vault at call time; the model never sees them.
  8. Observability. Log every tool call with agent ID, user ID, inputs and outputs. Alert on unusual volume, new tools, or data leaving.
  9. Kill switch. Disable an agent and revoke all its tokens in one step.

MCP security in practice

The Model Context Protocol connects agents to tool servers. Its authorization specification for remote servers is built on OAuth 2.1: clients discover the right authorization server from the MCP server's published metadata, tokens are issued for that specific server (its "audience"), and servers must not pass a client's token through to other APIs. Local MCP servers run on your machine with your permissions, so install only ones you trust, check what each can reach, and keep tool-call confirmations turned on for anything that writes or sends.

Module 28Frontier

AI for identity defenders, and shadow AI

Where AI helps the defender

  • Access reviews: recommending "revoke" based on peer-group comparison and actual usage, and explaining the risk in plain language so reviewers stop rubber-stamping.
  • Role mining and policy analysis: clustering permissions into sensible roles, finding toxic combinations, and answering "who can actually read this bucket?".
  • Detection and investigation: behaviour models (UEBA), turning plain-English questions into KQL or SPL, and stitching an incident timeline together across Entra, Okta, CloudTrail and SaaS logs.
  • Posture management: summarising thousands of ISPM findings into the ten that matter this week.

Where AI in identity decisions goes wrong

  • Confident wrong answers: a plausible but invented reason to approve access.
  • Learning the wrong "normal": a model trained on today's over-privileged access treats over-privilege as normal.
  • Automation without accountability: nobody can say who approved a privilege change.
  • Data exposure: identity data (who has access to what) is itself sensitive and shouldn't flow to unapproved AI tools.

Rule of thumb: AI recommends, a human approves any change to privileged access, and the AI's involvement is logged.

Shadow AI is an identity problem

Much of the enterprise risk from AI today isn't exotic. It's familiar identity sprawl in a new form:

  • Employees connect AI tools to Microsoft 365, Google Drive or Slack through OAuth consent, granting scopes like "read all mail" or "read all files" plus offline access.
  • AI browser extensions can read every page you open, including pages holding session data.
  • People paste secrets, customer data or tokens into chatbots.
  • Teams create personal API keys for AI services, which then leak into code and notebooks.

Controls: an admin-consent workflow for OAuth apps; an approved list of AI tools that use company SSO; secret scanning that recognises AI provider key formats; browser-extension management; and data-loss prevention for AI destinations.

Using Claude as your security copilot While you study, use Claude to explain log fields, draft KQL, run tabletop exercises, and critique your designs. Never paste real tokens, passwords, secrets, or customer or employee personal data. Use test tenants and made-up data.

Answers
  1. If the whole peer group is over-privileged, the model normalises it. Check usage data, not just similarity.
  2. Any two of: admin consent for OAuth apps, SSO-only approved AI tools, secret scanning for AI keys, browser-extension management, DLP.

Module 29Advanced

Cloud IAM and entitlement management

Every cloud has its own IAM system, and they are where most NHIs live. The core concepts, using AWS as the example:

  • Principals: users, roles (assumable identities with temporary credentials), and services.
  • Policies: JSON documents: Effect (Allow/Deny), Action, Resource, Condition. The subject-action-resource-condition sentence from Module 6, literally.
  • Evaluation logic: everything starts denied; an explicit Allow grants it; an explicit Deny anywhere always wins. Organization-wide guardrails (Service Control Policies) and permission boundaries cap what any policy can grant.
{ "Effect": "Allow",
  "Action": "s3:GetObject",
  "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::finance-reports/*",
  "Condition": { "Bool": { "aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent": "true" } } }

Azure uses role assignments (RBAC) at scopes (management group → subscription → resource group → resource). GCP binds roles to principals on resources in a hierarchy. Different syntax, same idea.

CIEM (Cloud Infrastructure Entitlement Management) tools analyze granted versus used permissions across clouds and find attack paths: chains like "this Lambda role can pass a role that can modify IAM". CIEM is now usually part of broader cloud security platforms (CNAPP).

Try it (30 min): In an AWS free-tier account, open IAM Access Analyzer and the "last accessed" information for a role. Or read three AWS-managed policies (like ReadOnlyAccess) and explain each in plain English.
Security lens

Attacker's view: Leaked access keys, privilege escalation through permissions like iam:PassRole, misconfigured cross-account trust, and stealing workload credentials from the instance metadata service via SSRF (the Capital One breach, 2019).

Detect: CloudTrail anomalies: new regions, unusual API calls, access keys used from unexpected networks. Cloud-native threat detection services flag many of these.

Defend: IMDSv2, organization-wide guardrail policies (SCPs), no long-term access keys, and CIEM to cut unused permissions.

2026-09-30 · Module 1

What is an identity? (first real session, not just setup)

  • A digital identity is four pieces: an entity (the real thing) plus identifiers (point to it), attributes (facts about it) and credentials (prove it). Nothing more exotic than that.
  • Two kinds of entity: human (interactive, can do MFA, eventually leaves) and non-human (automatic, can't do MFA, often outlives its creator). Machine identities now outnumber human ones by a wide margin, and get watched far less.
  • Identity became "the new perimeter" because the network edge stopped meaning anything once laptops, SaaS and cloud servers left the building — the one constant left in any access request is an identity asking for something.
  • The flip side: attackers don't break in any more, they log in.
  • The office-building analogy (entity=you, identifier=badge name, attributes=floor/department, credential=badge chip, reception=identity system) recurs through the whole course.

  • Goal: become an expert in identity security, both traditional (AD, SSO, IGA, PAM) and in the age of AI (deepfakes, AI agents, shadow AI).

  • Method: four evening sessions a week: read, explain back, security lens, lab. About 16 weeks, 33 modules.

Core concepts the mental models

Anatomy of an identity

Entity (the real thing) → identifiers (email, employee ID) + attributes (department, role) + credentials (password, passkey, certificate).

Two kinds of entity: humans and non-humans (service accounts, keys, workloads, bots, agents). NHIs now far outnumber humans.

Module 1

IAAA: the four questions

  1. Identification: who do you claim to be?
  2. Authentication (AuthN): prove it.
  3. Authorization (AuthZ): what may you do?
  4. Audit: what did you do?

AuthN ≠ AuthZ. Many breaches are authorization failures (too much access), not authentication failures.

Module 2

Tokens are wristbands

After you authenticate, you get a session cookie or token. Whoever holds it is treated as you, skipping the password and MFA. Token theft is the modern bypass.

Module 2

Who talks to whom

  • User → machine: login; phishing is the threat.
  • User → user: mediated by systems; impersonation, BEC and deepfakes are the threats.
  • Machine → machine: API keys, certificates, tokens; leaked long-lived secrets are the threat.
  • Machine on behalf of a user: delegation; over-scoped consent is the threat.

Human-present test: no human means no MFA, so rely on short lifetimes, vaults, workload identity and scoping.

Module 3

Delegation vs impersonation

Delegation: "Sam on behalf of Priya", with both identities logged, scoped and revocable. Impersonation: Sam acts as Priya and accountability is lost. Always prefer delegation, for people and for agents.

Module 3

Classifying credentials

  • Shared secret (password, API key): both sides know it, so either can leak it.
  • Key pair (passkey, certificate): the private key never leaves; the server stores only the public key.
  • Bearer (cookie, API key): works for whoever holds it.
  • Proof-of-possession (passkey, DPoP, mTLS): signs a fresh challenge, so it can't be replayed.

Primary → derived: passkey → IdP session → assertion or token → app cookie. Stealing a derived credential skips MFA.

Module 4

MFA strength ladder

  1. SMS / voice: SIM swap, phishable
  2. TOTP app: phishable (relayed)
  3. Push: MFA fatigue (number matching helps)
  4. FIDO2 / passkeys / certificates: phishing-resistant

Passkeys are phishing-resistant because the key is bound to the website's origin; a look-alike domain gets nothing.

Module 5

Where MFA can't reach

  • Legacy protocols (IMAP, POP, basic SMTP, NTLM)
  • After login (stolen tokens)
  • Machine identities
  • Enrolment and recovery (help desk, attacker-registered methods)
  • Inside the Windows network after desktop logon

Module 5

Authorization models

  • ACL: list on each resource
  • RBAC: roles (watch for role explosion)
  • ABAC: attributes and context
  • ReBAC: relationships (Google Zanzibar)
  • PBAC: policy as code (AWS IAM, OPA, Cedar)

Principles: least privilege, separation of duties, just-in-time, zero standing privilege.

Module 6

Directories and sources of truth

AD (on-prem: LDAP, Kerberos, Group Policy) ≠ Entra ID (cloud: SAML, OIDC, OAuth). Most enterprises are hybrid, synced by Entra Connect. HR is the authoritative source for people; the directory consumes it.

Module 7

Lifecycle: Joiner, Mover, Leaver

Mover is where privilege creep is born (new access is added, old access never removed). Leaver failures create orphaned accounts. SCIM automates provisioning and deprovisioning. NHIs rarely have a leaver event at all.

Module 9

SSO in one picture

Apps (SP / RP) outsource authentication to one IdP and trust its signed statement. Sessions come in layers (device, IdP, app), so disabling a user in the IdP doesn't kill existing app sessions. SSO creates accounts just-in-time but can't remove them, which is why SCIM is needed. Prefer SP-initiated flows. The IdP is the master key.

Module 10

IAM vs IGA vs PAM vs CIAM

  • IAM: make access work (AuthN, AuthZ, SSO)
  • IGA: keep access right, and prove it (reviews, SoD, lifecycle)
  • PAM: control the dangerous accounts (vault, rotation, session recording, JIT)
  • CIAM: customers at scale (low friction, bots, account takeover, privacy)

Modules 13–15

PAM vs PIM

PAM asks how a privileged account's credential is protected every time it's used (vault, rotate, record the session). PIM asks who currently holds a privileged role and for how long — eligible vs. active, time-boxed activation with justification (Entra ID's own PIM feature is the clearest real example). PIM governs who can become privileged; PAM protects the credential once they do. Both aim at the same goal: zero standing privilege.

Module 14

Attackers think in graphs

Stages: recon → initial access → persistence → privilege escalation → lateral movement → objectives. Almost every step is an identity action. BloodHound maps the graph of relationships to Domain Admin.

Module 17

Tier 0

Anything that controls identity for everything: DCs, AD FS and signing keys, Entra Connect, AD CS, PAM vault, IdP super-admins, device-management admins. Tier 0 accounts log in only to Tier 0 systems, from PAWs.

Modules 17, 19

Zero Trust, ISPM, ITDR

Zero Trust (NIST 800-207): never trust, always verify, every request. ISPM: finds standing weaknesses (posture). ITDR: detects and responds to active identity attacks. Shared Signals / CAEP: real-time "revoke this session" between systems.

Module 18

Protocol cheat sheets purpose · flow · attacks · detect · defend

NTLM

Legacy Windows challenge-response authentication. Deprecated; NTLMv1 is removed from the newest Windows versions.

Flow
NEGOTIATE → CHALLENGE (random number) → AUTHENTICATE (response computed from the NT hash); the server asks the DC to verify.
Weaknesses
NT hash is as good as the password; no server authentication; no MFA.
Attacks
Pass-the-hash, NTLM relay (with coercion tricks such as PetitPotam), offline cracking.
Detect
4776 NTLM validations where Kerberos is expected; 4624 logon type 3 with NTLM from unusual hosts.
Defend
Audit, then restrict NTLM; SMB signing; LDAP signing and channel binding; EPA; Protected Users group.

Module 8

Kerberos

Ticket-based network authentication through a trusted third party (the KDC on every domain controller).

Flow
AS-REQ (pre-auth) → AS-REP (TGT, sealed with the krbtgt key) → TGS-REQ (for an SPN) → TGS-REP (service ticket, sealed with the service key) → AP-REQ → optional AP-REP (mutual authentication).
Key facts
The PAC carries group memberships; tickets last about 10 hours; clocks must be within 5 minutes; PKINIT adds smart cards and Windows Hello; constrained delegation is OK, unconstrained is dangerous.
Attacks
Kerberoasting, AS-REP roasting, pass-the-ticket, Silver Ticket, Golden Ticket, delegation abuse.
Detect
4769 with RC4 (0x17) across many SPNs; 4768 / 4771 anomalies; TGS without a prior TGT; ticket lifetimes that don't match policy.
Defend
gMSA, AES only, pre-auth on, Credential Guard, Tier 0 isolation, reset krbtgt twice after compromise.

Module 8

SAML 2.0

XML-based browser SSO for enterprise web apps (OASIS, 2005).

Setup
Exchange metadata: IdP entity ID, SSO URL and signing certificate ↔ SP entity ID and ACS URL.
Flow
SP → browser redirect with AuthnRequest → IdP login and MFA → signed SAMLResponse auto-POSTed to the ACS URL → SP session. All front channel.
Assertion
Issuer (≈ iss), NameID (≈ sub), Audience (≈ aud), NotOnOrAfter (≈ exp), AuthnContext (≈ amr/acr), attributes.
SP must check
Signature against the stored IdP certificate, issuer, audience, time window, InResponseTo, destination, replay.
Attacks
Golden SAML (stolen signing key), XML signature wrapping, comment injection, weak validation, certificate-expiry outages.
Detect
App sign-ins with no matching IdP sign-in; signing-certificate changes; AD FS configuration exports.
Defend
HSM or cloud IdP for signing keys; mature libraries; SP-initiated flows; certificate expiry tracking.

Module 11

OAuth 2.0

Delegated authorization for APIs. Not authentication.

Grants
Authorization code + PKCE (user present); client credentials (machine to machine); refresh token; device code; token exchange (on behalf of).
Tokens
Access token (minutes to hours, scoped); refresh token (days to months; very valuable).
Attacks
Consent phishing, device-code phishing, refresh-token theft, redirect-URI manipulation, wrong-audience acceptance, leaked client secrets.
Detect
New high-risk consents (Mail.Read, offline_access); unusual device-code sign-ins; new app secrets.
Defend
Admin consent workflow; PKCE; exact redirect URIs; sender-constrained tokens (DPoP, mTLS); certificates or federation instead of secrets; follow the OAuth Security BCP (RFC 9700).

Module 12

OpenID Connect (OIDC)

Authentication layer on top of OAuth 2.0. Adds the ID token (a JWT).

Flow
Authorization code + PKCE; tokens are fetched over the back channel, server to server.
ID token claims
iss, sub, aud, exp, iat, nonce, amr / acr (how the user authenticated).
Validate
Signature (via the IdP's JWKS), issuer, audience, expiry, nonce.
Remember
A JWT is signed, not encrypted: anyone can read it, so no secrets inside.

Module 12

FIDO2 / WebAuthn / passkeys

Phishing-resistant public-key authentication.

Parts
WebAuthn (browser API, W3C) + CTAP (talks to security keys and phones, FIDO Alliance).
Why it works
The key pair is bound to the website's origin, so the device won't sign for a look-alike site; the server stores only public keys.
Watch
Synced passkeys depend on the security of the cloud account they sync through; recovery flows can reintroduce weak links.

Module 5

TOTP

Time-based one-time passwords (RFC 6238), used by authenticator apps.

How
code = last 6 digits of HMAC(shared secret, time ÷ 30 s). Both sides compute it; nothing is sent.
Weakness
Doesn't know which site it's typed into (relayable); a leaked seed or QR code means codes forever.

Module 5

SCIM 2.0

Standard REST API for provisioning and deprovisioning accounts (RFC 7643 / 7644).

Why
SSO can't remove accounts; SCIM lets the IdP create, update and disable users and groups in apps automatically.

Module 9

Other protocols

RADIUS
VPN, Wi-Fi and network devices; the classic place to add MFA to a VPN.
TACACS+
Admin access to network devices, with command logging.
802.1X / EAP
Network access control; best with device certificates (EAP-TLS).
LDAP bind
An app checks passwords against the directory. Not SSO: the app sees your password.
WS-Federation
Older Microsoft web SSO (AD FS).
SSH
Keys or, better, short-lived SSH certificates.

Module 10

Workload identity (SPIFFE / cloud federation)

Machines prove what they are through their platform, with no stored secret.

Pattern
The platform (GitHub Actions, Kubernetes, a cloud VM) issues a signed token about the workload → it's exchanged for short-lived credentials scoped to exactly that workload.
Examples
AWS IAM roles, Azure managed identities, GCP workload identity federation, GitHub Actions OIDC, SPIFFE / SPIRE SVIDs (X.509 or JWT).

Module 24

MCP authorization

How AI agents (MCP clients) get access to remote tool servers.

Basis
OAuth 2.1; the client discovers the authorization server from the MCP server's protected-resource metadata (RFC 9728).
Rules
Tokens are issued for that specific server (audience / resource indicators, RFC 8707); servers must not pass client tokens through to other APIs.
Local servers
Run with your own permissions: install only trusted ones and keep confirmations on for anything that writes.

Incident response playbooks evict, don't just reset

Compromised user account

  1. Block sign-in; revoke sessions and refresh tokens; reset password
  2. Remove persistence: MFA methods, OAuth consents, app passwords, inbox rules, devices, PATs, SSH keys, new accounts
  3. Scope with sign-in and audit logs
  4. Verify the user out-of-band; re-enrol with FIDO2; clean the device
  5. Fix the root cause

Stolen token or session

  • Revoke sessions
  • Find the infostealer; reimage the device
  • Require compliant devices; shorten sessions; token protection

Leaked secret or key

  • Rotate and revoke first
  • Search logs for use of the old key
  • Hunt for other leaks in the same repo or pipeline
  • Verify every copy was rotated (the Cloudflare lesson)

Compromised IdP admin

  • Act from break-glass accounts
  • Check federation, IdP routing, new apps and secrets, policy changes, role grants
  • Revoke all admin sessions

AD domain compromise

  • Assume total compromise; scope quietly
  • Reset krbtgt twice, waiting for replication and ticket expiry in between
  • Reset all privileged and service accounts; check AD CS
  • Possibly rebuild DCs or recover the forest; evict in one coordinated event

Third-party integration breach

  • Revoke the vendor's tokens and keys
  • Review what data it accessed
  • Re-grant with reduced scopes

Cisco Duo: Identity Security (formerly "just MFA")

Duo's own pitch: it evolved from an MFA tool into a full security-first IAM platform — directory, SSO, passwordless, device trust, automation — without replacing a company's existing IdP.

Deployment modes
Three ways to sit in an environment: standalone IAM for companies with no IdP; an identity broker in front of an existing IdP (Entra, Okta, Ping); or an alternate directory just for third parties (contractors, vendors) who shouldn't join the main corporate directory.
True passwordless
Duo Directory removes passwords entirely, including from enrollment and account-recovery flows — the password never exists to begin with, not just "hidden" behind MFA. Works via Duo Directory itself, or synced external directories (Entra, SAML 2.0, on-prem AD). Embedded browsers that don't support WebAuthn get bridged through the Duo Desktop app instead.
Session token theft defense ("Duo Passport")
Strong device binding: the endpoint signs a user+device profile and sends it to Duo's cloud on every app access; if a profile doesn't match what was recorded, the session is treated as suspect. Because the binding is to the device, not just the token, Duo can invalidate a stolen session cookie even after an attacker-in-the-middle (AiTM) phishing attack has already captured it.
Device trust
Two separate checks: device health (disk encryption, firewall, password set, OS version, 3rd-party security agent, browser/plugin versions — a posture check) and trusted endpoints (does this exact device identifier appear in Duo's device inventory at all — a allow-list check, independent of health).
AD Defense — closing the legacy gap
Most legacy on-prem apps only authenticate via Kerberos or NTLM and can't natively support MFA. Duo's AD Defense inserts itself at the domain controller via a subauthentication filter (a DLL) and intercepts successful Kerberos/NTLM logons before granting access, adding MFA to authentication protocols that were never designed to support it. A separate CLI-protection agent does the same for Windows SSH, sFTP and remote PowerShell specifically — the protocols attackers use for lateral movement and privilege escalation.
Provisioning
SCIM both directions: inbound from Okta/Google Directory to build Duo's own user database, outbound from Duo to downstream apps (Secure Access, Webex). This is the practical mechanism behind the course's lifecycle/SCIM material (Module 9) — a real product doing exactly that create/update/delete flow.

Cisco Identity Intelligence (CII, formerly Oort): ITDR and ISPM in practice

The course names ITDR and ISPM as concepts (Module 18) without a concrete product behind them. This is that product — a real implementation of "detect and respond to identity attacks" and "find standing posture weaknesses" as an acquired, shipping tool.

The problem it exists to solve
Most organizations run multiple IdPs at once (Okta, Entra, Duo SSO), each with its own logs and its own partial view. An IdP can only detect what it itself sees — it has no visibility into a different IdP's authentication events for the same user. CII's core pitch is correlation: aggregate authentication events across every IdP into one analytical system, which both surfaces attacks a single IdP would miss and reduces false positives from noisy single-source alerts.
The identity graph
CII builds a graph connecting users, devices, sessions, groups, apps and infrastructure, fed from identity providers, HRIS systems, MDM, and applications — via full inventory sync, delta sync (changed-records-only), or (preferred) near-real-time event streaming. Streaming matters because it's materially faster: a compromised-session detection that's near-instant over streaming can take over 24 hours via periodic API sync — and in that same window, Entra's own alerting can label a real, successful compromise as only "Medium" criticality.
"Checks"
CII's term for a detection rule — explicitly compared to IDS signatures. When collected data matches a check's condition, that check is recorded as failed for that user/account. Checks are tagged to risk frameworks (CIS, NIST, MITRE ATT&CK) and filterable by which IdP they apply to, since not every check works against every provider's data.
Identity Security Posture Management (ISPM) in practice
An organization gets a posture score, benchmarked against similar organizations, tracked as a trend over time — the proactive half of the pitch. Concrete checks it runs: priority accounts (admins) missing MFA, accounts that have never logged in, and tracking real authentication-factor usage against what the organization believes it's enforcing — one quoted example: a CISO certain SMS-based MFA had been fully retired was shown, via the dashboard, that it hadn't been.
Session hijacking detection — a worked AiTM example
The deck walks an Evilginx-style attacker-in-the-middle attack step by step: phishing email → realistic fake Microsoft login page → attacker-controlled proxy captures username, password, and the live MFA-approved session cookie → attacker replays that cookie to access the account, fully bypassing MFA. This is the exact mechanism behind the "stolen session cookie" line in this KB's attack matrix, now with the real product-detection angle: event-streaming from the IdP is what lets CII catch this quickly; without it, detection can take over a day. By default CII runs its checks once daily; "Threat Discovery Mode" can raise that to hourly, but only once streaming and a notification target are configured.
User Trust Level
A single score per user, built from posture, behavior and events over time — explicitly not a real-time risk score, but a longer-horizon approximation other integrated systems (XDR, Secure Access) can act on.
Active Directory specifically
90% of enterprises still run AD. Per Cisco Talos incident-response data cited in the deck, 44% of identity-based attacks specifically target AD — multiple domains, legacy integrations, and generations of accumulated privileged accounts make it a dense attack surface. CII's AD-focused checks include flagging "never logged in" accounts (one customer cleaned up 150 stale AD accounts from this single check) and correlating group membership for privilege-creep detection.

Source: Cisco Live BRKSEC-2162, "Identity Intelligence Demystified," 2026. See Module 18 (ITDR, ISPM) and the attack → detect → defend matrix's session-hijacking entries.
